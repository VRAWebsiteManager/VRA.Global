Key Person Risk in a Business Sale

You can usually read it off the file in the first ten minutes. Revenue is steady, the customers are loyal, the books are clean enough — and one person knows how all of it actually works. Every professional on the deal can see it. None of them is the one whose job it is to fix it.

Key person risk is what that gap gets called once it reaches a term sheet, and the cost lands in a different place depending on where you sit: a discount in the valuation conversation, a note still outstanding years after everyone else was paid, or a recommendation that was written up, agreed to, and never acted on. This is what it does to a deal, and what it takes to close it.

What Does Key Person Risk Cost a Deal?

A buyer is not paying for last year’s revenue. They are paying for next year’s, and the question underneath every diligence request is whether next year arrives without the seller in the building. Anything a buyer cannot verify, they assume the worst about and price accordingly.

In practice it surfaces as a lower offer, more of the price tied to how the business performs after closing, a longer transition period demanded of the seller, or a buyer who simply stops returning calls after diligence. It rarely arrives as a stated objection, which is exactly what makes it expensive: owner dependence is the quietest thing on the file, and nobody has to name it out loud to charge for it.

Why Does Nobody Fix It?

Not because it is hard to diagnose. Because of where each party is standing when the problem comes due.

  • The broker is paid at closing, and the condition predates the listing. Raising it means asking a seller to spend months on a project before going to market.
  • The lender carries it longest and controls it least. Everyone else is paid at close, while the note runs on against a business that has just lost the only person who knew how it ran.
  • The advisor already named it. The assessment found the gap, the owner agreed with every word, and the only person who could run the project is the one person in the company with no spare hours.
  • The owner is the constraint and the fix at once. Which is why the recommendation tends to go quiet rather than get refused.

So the gap survives diligence, survives closing, and turns up again in the first ninety days as the buyer’s problem.

What Would Actually Close the Gap?

Four things, and the owner has none of them spare: somebody whose actual job it is, hours that already exist rather than a hire, documentation produced while the work is being done rather than at an offsite, and a stretch where the owner stepped back and the business kept moving. That last one is the only part a buyer or a credit committee can see for themselves.

A trained VRA assistant is the hands for that. They sit alongside the owner, record how the work is actually done, exceptions included, and turn it into standard operating procedures that belong to the company rather than to anyone’s memory. Then they administer the daily back office — phones during business hours, calendar, invoicing, follow-up, the receivables nobody has chased — so the owner can step back far enough for the stepping back to mean something. Decisions still route to the owner. The routine work stops doing so.

The timing is the part that matters to a deal. An assistant can start with the seller before the business is listed, and the engagement can transfer to the buyer at closing, so the same person continues with the new owner instead of leaving with the seller. The procedures belong to the business either way. What transfers at close is a written back office and somebody already administering it, rather than two weeks of the seller trying to remember everything.

Questions Deal Professionals Ask

Do you advise on valuation, deal structure or timing?

No. VRA provides administrative hands, not advice: no assessments, scores or readiness reports, no valuation views, and no guidance on structure, timing or tax. Whatever makes your work yours stays yours.

Does the engagement transfer to the buyer at closing?

Yes. The same assistant can continue with the new owner. VRA’s only commitment is a 3-month minimum, and terms can be adjusted to fit the deal. Who pays, and from when, is set by the seller and the buyer in the purchase agreement or transition plan.

Is this billed to my firm?

No. The seller or the borrower pays, depending on when it starts. It is never billed to a lender, a brokerage or an advisory firm, and we do not ask you to carry any part of it.

Does the assistant manage the seller’s staff?

No. An assistant administers the back office; they do not supervise anyone and they are not a manager. The point is to take administrative work off the people the business needs doing the actual work.

Does this only work in certain industries?

No. Assistants are trained as generalists and matched to the business. Documenting how a company operates is much the same work whether the target is a restaurant, a contractor, a distributor or a professional practice.

These answers are general guidance, not legal, tax or financial advice. The specifics of any deal sit with the parties and their own counsel.
