How Much Does a Virtual Assistant Cost? An Honest 2026 Breakdown

The short answer: on a freelance marketplace, general admin listings sit around $10 to $20 an hour. Through an agency selling blocks of hours, published plans work out to roughly $35 to $65 an hour or more. Buy a monthly placement instead and you are looking at about $500 to $3,000 a month part-time, or $1,000 to $5,000 and up full-time. The reason those numbers are so far apart is that “virtual assistant” covers four different ways of buying help, and each one prices differently.

Here is what each model costs, what drives the number, what gets left out of it, and how to compare two quotes fairly.

What Does a Virtual Assistant Cost, by Model?

These are the ranges you’ll see in public listings and provider pricing pages in 2026. They are market ranges, not quotes, and any single provider can sit above or below them.

Freelancer, paid hourly

Freelance marketplaces show the lowest headline rates. For general admin, listings sit around $10 to $20 an hour, with specialized support such as bookkeeping or marketing higher. The rate buys hours and nothing else: you write the job post, screen the applicants, train the person you pick, manage the work and start over if it doesn’t work out. A marketplace may also add a platform fee to each payment. This model can suit a defined, short project. It asks more of your time than the other models when you need the same person on the same work every week.

Agency, hour blocks or tiers

A traditional VA agency may sell a block of hours per month, such as 45 to 60 hours, at a fixed fee. Divided out, published plans of this kind work out to roughly $35 to $65 an hour or more. You get some matching and support, but you also take on two risks: hours beyond the block cost extra, and tiers named “basic,” “managed” or “specialized” may leave you to guess which one your work belongs in. Smaller blocks can carry a higher hourly rate than larger ones.

Monthly placement

In this model, you get one assistant on a set schedule for a monthly fee, part-time or full-time. Published monthly figures run from about $500 to $3,000 for part-time and $1,000 to $5,000 or more for full-time. The headline matters less here than what sits around it: setup fees, minimum terms, whether the assistant is shared across clients, what happens if the fit is wrong, and whether industry knowledge costs extra.

In-house hire

The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the median salary for secretaries and administrative assistants at $48,310 a year, or $23.23 an hour, as of May 2025. That is the salary line only. A payroll hire also carries employer payroll taxes, the benefits you offer, equipment, paid time off, and the cost of recruiting and replacing someone if the first hire doesn’t work out. What those add depends on what you offer and where you are, so we won’t put a percentage on them. They are additions to $48,310, not part of it. What Drives the Price?

Some of the spread is real. Before you compare quotes, know what moves the number:

  • Who does the hiring work. A marketplace rate leaves the screening, training, management and replacement to you. An agency or placement rate includes some or all of it. Comparing them on hourly rate alone compares a part to a whole.
  • One named assistant or a shared pool. A named assistant learns your business. A pooled model draws from a shared team, so the person handling your work on Thursday may not be the one who handled it on Monday. Pooled can be cheaper per hour and more expensive in rework.
  • Hours and commitment. Small blocks of hours can carry a higher hourly rate. Longer commitments can bring a lower one.
  • Type of work. General admin is priced below specialized support such as bookkeeping or marketing.
  • Industry tiers. A provider may price industry knowledge, including real estate, as a tier or an upcharge. That is where the tier problem turns into real money.

Which Costs Don’t Show Up in the Rate?

The quoted figure and the amount you pay can be two different numbers. Check for:

  • Setup or onboarding fees charged before any work starts.
  • Overage charges when a busy month runs past your block of hours.
  • Platform fees added to each freelance payment.
  • Specialization upcharges that appear once your work is reviewed.
  • Starting-at prices. “Starting at” is a floor, not a price. The real figure can arrive after a strategy call.
  • Your own time. Screening, training, checking work and replacing someone who doesn’t fit all come out of your week. That time has a cost even if no one invoices it.
  • Replacement terms. A guarantee limited to a trial window can leave you paying for a poor fit after it closes.
  • Payroll extras on an in-house hire, covered above.

The largest of those is usually your own time, and it is the one no quote shows. Who pays to train your virtual assistant works through where that cost lands in each hiring model.

How Do You Compare Any Two Quotes?

Put every option on the same footing: one month, a defined number of hours, everything included. Then ask each provider, in writing:

  • Is this the price, or the starting price?
  • Is the assistant assigned to us, or shared across clients?
  • Are there setup, onboarding or overage fees?
  • Does the rate change if our work needs industry knowledge?
  • What is the minimum commitment?
  • What happens, and what does it cost, if the person is not the right fit?

A provider who can answer all six in writing, before a call, is quoting you a price. A provider who needs a call first is quoting you a conversation. If you’re not sure how many hours you need, write down what you’d hand off first.
