Policy Checking, Endorsements and Renewal Prep: What an Insurance Assistant Does

The short answer: an insurance agency assistant carries the service-account workload — checking issued policies against what was quoted, preparing endorsement requests and tracking them to confirmation, issuing and chasing certificates, and building the renewal file before the producer needs it. The coverage decisions, the advice and the binding stay with your licensed staff. This walks the work stage by stage, starting with the piece most agencies know they are not doing properly.

Why Does Policy Checking Get Skipped?

Ask an agency owner whether every issued policy gets checked against the quote and you tend to get a pause. It is not that anyone disagrees with doing it. It is that checking a policy properly takes an uninterrupted half hour, produces nothing visible when it finds nothing, and competes with a service queue that is making noise right now.

So it gets done on the accounts that feel risky and skipped on the rest. And the errors it would have caught are not exotic: a limit typed wrong, an endorsement that was quoted and never issued, a named insured that does not match the entity on the contract, an effective date a day off from the expiring policy. None of those announce themselves. The agency finds out at claim time, which is the worst moment available and the one where the conversation turns to who was supposed to check.

This is not peculiar to insurance. A CPA, thirty days into working with a VRA assistant, described the same problem in one sentence:

“He’s helped me make sure things that have been falling through the cracks no longer fall through the cracks, and keeping me honest on everything that I have going on.”
— Austin Galvez, CPA, Ratio Fortis · 30-day check-in

Which Three Documents Does Policy Checking Compare?

An issued policy gets compared against three things, and the common shortcut is to use only the second:

  • The application — what the client said about the risk
  • The quote or proposal — what was promised, and what it was priced at
  • The prior declarations page — on a renewal, what was actually in force last term

Where those three disagree with each other, that is worth flagging as well. It usually means something changed mid-process and not everybody got the memo, which is a different problem from a carrier keying error and needs a different person to resolve it.

The Field-by-Field Pass

Worked properly this is the same order every time, because consistency is what makes it reliable rather than a reading exercise:

  • Named insured — exact legal name and the right entity type, since an LLC and an Inc are not a typo apart, they are different legal persons
  • Mailing address and location addresses
  • Policy period — effective and expiration dates, and whether a renewal leaves a gap
  • Coverage parts present — is anything that was quoted missing entirely
  • Limits and deductibles — every one, against the quote
  • Forms and endorsements — listed on the dec page, matched to the quote
  • Additional insureds, mortgagees, lienholders and loss payees
  • Premium, against what was quoted
  • Exposure basis where it drives the premium — payroll, sales, vehicle count

Flag, Don’t Fix

The whole task sits on one distinction. Describing a difference is clerical. Deciding what should be there is licensed work.

“The quote shows a $1,000,000 per-occurrence limit and the policy shows $500,000” is the assistant’s to say. “You need the $1,000,000 limit” is the producer’s. So the assistant logs the discrepancy, attaches both documents, assigns it to the account manager and states plainly what differs from what. They do not call the carrier to change coverage on their own authority and they do not correct the policy themselves.

And when it is unclear whether something counts as a discrepancy, the instruction is to flag it anyway. A false flag costs an account manager thirty seconds. A missed one costs a claim.

Endorsements, Certificates and the Service Queue

Policy checking is the part nobody gets to. The service queue is the part nobody escapes. An assistant takes the repeatable half of it: preparing endorsement requests for addition, deletion and change, submitting them through your approved channels, and — the step that actually goes missing — tracking each one to confirmation rather than assuming the carrier processed it.

Certificates work the same way: issuance from your standing templates, holder and expiration tracking, and the chasing that happens when a client’s vendor wants an updated certificate by Friday. Unusual wording, anything that changes coverage, and the decision to bind stay with your licensed team, which is also where a certificate request stops being clerical.

One operational habit is worth stealing whatever you decide about outsourcing: agree in writing what a finished request looks like. Most service backlogs are not a volume problem, they are a definition problem — requests sit in a state nobody can name, where it is unclear whether the agency is waiting on the carrier, the client, or itself.

The Renewal Runway

Renewals reward working backwards. Expiration lists built far enough ahead to leave room, updated exposure information collected from the client, loss runs ordered and chased, and the renewal file assembled so the producer has a complete picture before the marketing conversation rather than during it. When the renewal policy arrives, it goes through the same field pass against the expiring one.

Strategy, the decision to remarket and the client conversation belong to the producer. What the assistant removes is the two-week scramble before expiration, which is where most agencies lose the chance to do anything other than renew as-is.

Questions Agencies Ask About Policy Checking

What is policy checking?

Comparing the policy the carrier actually issued against what was asked for and what was quoted, field by field, and flagging anything that differs. Carriers make mistakes: a limit gets typed wrong, an endorsement that was quoted never makes it onto the policy, a named insured is spelled a way that does not match the entity on the contract, an effective date lands a day off. If nobody checks, the agency usually finds out at claim time, which is the worst moment available.

What does an issued policy get checked against?

Three documents, not one. The application, for what the client said about the risk. The quote or proposal, for what was promised and what it was priced at. And on a renewal, the prior declarations page, for what was actually in force last term. Checking only against the quote is the common shortcut and it misses the most expensive errors. Where the three disagree with each other, that is worth flagging too — it usually means something changed mid-process and not everyone got the memo.

What happens when an assistant finds a discrepancy?

It gets logged and assigned, not fixed. Describing a difference is clerical work; deciding what should be there is licensed work. So the assistant records what differs from what, attaches both documents and assigns it to the account manager or producer — they do not call the carrier to change coverage, and they do not correct the policy. When in doubt about whether something is a discrepancy, the instruction is to flag it: a false flag costs an account manager thirty seconds, and a missed one costs a claim.

Can an assistant issue certificates of insurance and process endorsements?

Yes, within your approved procedures. Certificate issuance from standing templates, tracking holders and expirations, chasing the renewal certificates your clients’ vendors ask for, and preparing endorsement requests — additions, deletions and changes — for submission and then tracking them to confirmation. Unusual certificate wording, anything that changes coverage, and the decision to bind stay with your licensed team.

How does renewal prep work?

Backwards from the expiration date. Expiration lists built far enough ahead to be useful, updated exposure information collected from the client, loss runs ordered and chased, renewal files assembled so the producer has a complete picture before the marketing conversation, and the renewal policy checked against the expiring one when it arrives. Renewal strategy, the decision to remarket and the client conversation belong to the producer; what the assistant removes is the scramble in the two weeks before expiration.

What should an agency measure in the first 60 days?

Pick the numbers you already complain about. Average age of open service requests, the share of requests completed without a correction, how long a certificate takes from request to delivery, how many renewals are worked more than thirty days out, and discrepancies caught before the policy is delivered rather than after. VRA checks in at 14, 30, 60 and 90 days, which is a reasonable place to look at the same figures together.
