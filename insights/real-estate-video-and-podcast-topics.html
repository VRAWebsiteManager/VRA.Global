What to Film and What to Record

The short answer: the hard part was never the camera. It is deciding what to say, and deciding it again every week. An office that keeps a list of topics on hand turns a standing decision into a standing appointment — an agent arrives knowing what they are making, and nothing has to be invented in the moment.

What follows is a bank to start from. All of it is the kind of thing an agent already explains out loud several times a month, and all of it can be recorded sitting in a room.

Start Narrower Than Feels Comfortable

Before working through the list, pick the thing to be known for. An agent who covers everything is remembered for nothing. A few neighborhoods, a kind of client, one part of the process — first-time buyers, relocation, probate, a single zip code, investors working one asset class.

Narrow is easier, not harder. The questions repeat, so there is always a next topic and the answers are already known. And it is how people find them: somebody looking for help with a specific thing finds the person who talks about that thing.

It is not a life sentence. The focus moves as a business does, and most do. What matters is that it is narrow enough at any one moment to be recognizable, rather than broad enough to be forgettable.

The Market Where They Actually Work

The most valuable category and the most neglected one, because it is the only material an agent somewhere else cannot make. When somebody wants to understand a place, they go looking for a person talking about it, and whoever is talking becomes the expert.

  • What a neighborhood is actually like to live in
  • What has changed on a street in five years, and why
  • Why one block holds value and the next one does not
  • What a commute really costs in time
  • Which streets flood and which do not
  • What is being built, and what it will do to the area
  • What a budget buys here against the neighborhood next to it
  • Where people actually eat, and what a weekend looks like

The Questions That Come Up in Every First Meeting

Every agent answers the same handful of questions over and over, which means they can already explain each one well. Answered once, properly, each one stays useful for years.

  • What actually happens between an accepted offer and closing
  • What an inspection does and does not cover
  • How an appraisal gap gets handled
  • What a seller should fix, and what they should leave alone
  • What the costs are on each side, named plainly
  • What a pre-approval proves and what it does not
  • How showings work now
  • Why a listing sat, and what fixed it

What the Office Just Generated

An agent who has just sat through something has more to say than one asked to think of a topic from nothing, and the material has a date on it, which is its own reason to publish. Turning office events into agent content covers this one in full.

  • A builder presenting a new incentive
  • A lender explaining a rate program
  • A title officer walking through a change
  • A class the office just ran, and what people took from it
  • A market shift clients are already asking about
  • What an agent brought back from a national event

The People Clients Already Rely On

The conversation is useful to the audience and the relationship is useful afterwards. These are also the easiest invitations to accept, because the guest gets something out of it too.

  • A lender on what buyers get wrong about rates
  • An inspector on what they find most often
  • A title officer on what delays a closing
  • A contractor on what a renovation really costs this year
  • A designer on what sells and what does not
  • A local business owner on the neighborhood they have worked in for twenty years

The Work Itself

This category feels self-indulgent and is not. People hire a person, and this is the material that shows what that person is like to deal with.

  • What an agent does all week that clients never see
  • What they learned from a deal that went sideways
  • A decision a client faced, and how it was weighed
  • What they would tell themselves starting out
  • Why they work the way they do

Which Ones Want a Camera

Short pieces where the agent’s delivery carries it — explaining how something works, answering a question clients keep asking, reacting to something the market just did. Anything an agent wants to send to one client and post for everybody else.

Audio suits the opposite: subjects that take time, conversations with another person, and anything somebody would rather listen to while driving through the neighborhood being discussed. A twenty-minute explanation nobody would watch is one somebody will happily hear on a commute.

The full arguments are in how video builds a real estate business and how a podcast builds a real estate business.

Working Through the List

Pick the next three rather than the next one. An agent who leaves a session knowing what the following two are does not have to restart the decision, and that is usually what decides whether there is a second piece at all.

Better still, make the three a series on one narrow subject — what a first-time buyer runs into, one neighborhood from five angles, a single step of the process taken apart. A series compounds: somebody who finds one piece has a reason to watch the next two, and the agent becomes associated with the subject rather than with having made some videos. Three unrelated pieces are three separate starts.

Sessions do not have to be one at a time either. An agent with a room booked and somebody running it can record several in an afternoon and be weeks ahead.

Common Questions

Is a series better than one-off pieces?

Yes. Three pieces on one narrow subject build on each other and give somebody who finds the first a reason to stay for the rest. Three unrelated pieces are three separate starts, and none of them makes the agent known for anything in particular.

Can an agent change their focus later?

Yes, and most do as the business changes. Starting narrow is about being recognizable for something now rather than forgettable across everything. A focus that moves in a year has still done its job.

What should an agent make first?

Something from the market where they work. It is the material nobody else can make, it needs no preparation beyond knowing the area, and it reaches the people who are actually going to hire them.

How many topics does an office need on hand?

Enough that no one has to invent one in the moment. The list matters less than the fact that it exists before the session rather than during it.

Which topics work better as video than as audio?

Short pieces where the agent’s delivery carries it — explaining something, answering a question, reacting to a change. Audio suits subjects that take time to explain and conversations with another person, which is most of what an agent knows and almost none of what fits in thirty seconds.

Does an agent need a guest?

No. Guests add reach and relationships, but every category above works solo. A show or a series that already exists is also a much easier thing to invite someone onto later.

What makes an agent stop after the first one?

Not the first one being bad. It is that making the next one went back to being their job — deciding the topic, writing it, shooting it, editing it. A list and a booked room remove the first and the last of those.
