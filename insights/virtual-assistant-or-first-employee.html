Growing a Business

Virtual Assistant or First Employee?

There is a stretch in the life of a growing small business where the owner is doing two jobs badly instead of one job well. The work that brings money in competes every day with the work that keeps the lights on, and the admin usually wins, because it is louder. Nobody chases you about the proposal you did not write. They do chase you about the invoice you did not send.

Owners need help long before they can afford a full-time employee. You know you have outgrown doing everything yourself, and you also know the payroll math does not work yet. That gap — needing to hire help you cannot yet justify hiring — is the subject of this piece.

When Should You Hire Help? The Threshold Is Not About Size

The signal that you have outgrown doing everything yourself is not volume. It is slippage. Quotes go out two days late. Calls get returned tomorrow. Invoices sit unchased for a month because chasing them is unpleasant and never urgent until it is. The scheduling gets done at nine at night, which means it gets done badly.

At that stage the admin is no longer costing you time. It is costing you revenue, because the hours it eats are the hours you would have spent selling, bidding, or building the thing people pay for. That is the threshold. It has nothing to do with headcount or turnover, and it arrives for a two-person operation and a twenty-person one alike.

Not sure what you would hand off? The delegation quiz takes about two minutes and comes back with a specific recommendation.

The Ceiling Is Made of Hours

In a business where the owner generates the work, growth creates the thing that stops it. The jobs coming in this month are the return on the selling, bidding and following up you did two months ago. Servicing them takes exactly the hours that used to go into the selling, bidding and following up.

So production flattens. Not because demand dried up, and not because anything went wrong — because the person generating it ran out of day. The business is busy and it is no longer growing, which is the most frustrating version of the problem, because from the inside it feels like working harder should fix it.

It does not, and the owners who get past it are the ones who move the coordination, the paperwork and the follow-up off their own desk before it is comfortable to do so.

That is what a VRA assistant is for: the administrative load that grew with the business and now competes with it.

What Should You Delegate, and What Needs a Hire?

The useful question is not which option is better. It is which bucket the work falls into — what you can delegate to an assistant, and what genuinely needs an employee.

An assistant handles work that is repeatable, can be written down, and happens at a desk. Scheduling and calendar management. Phone and inbox coverage. Invoicing, and chasing what is owed. CRM and database upkeep. Document collection and client follow-up. Reporting. Marketing and social coordination. Writing down the procedures nobody has ever written down.

A hire is for work that needs a person in the room, a professional license, or authority over other people. Anyone running your floor during service, holding a license to sign something, or supervising staff needs to be your employee. A VRA assistant administers the back office; they do not run your business and they do not manage your people.

A third bucket is worth naming: work nobody has defined. If you cannot describe the task well enough to write it down, neither an assistant nor an employee will do it the way you want. Defining it is the prerequisite, not the delegation.

What Do the Two Cost?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the median wage for secretaries and administrative assistants at $48,310 a year, or $23.23 an hour, as of May 2025. A full-time VRA assistant is $1,500 a month — $18,000 a year — for the same forty hours a week. Part-time is $800 a month for twenty.

The salary is also the smallest part of the payroll figure. Employer payroll taxes, benefits, equipment, paid time off, and the cost of recruiting and replacing a bad first hire all sit on top of $48,310. We are not going to invent a percentage for them, because it depends on what you offer and where you are, but they are additions to that number and there is nothing to add to ours.

The purchases are genuinely different, and that is worth saying plainly. An employee is yours: you recruit them, train them, and carry the relationship. A VRA assistant arrives having completed a mandatory four-week instructor-led training program, is matched to you rather than assigned from a queue, and comes with check-ins at 14, 30, 60 and 90 days and a free swap if the fit is wrong. You are buying administrative capacity, not headcount. The full breakdown is in what a virtual assistant costs, and if AI is the third option you are weighing, that comparison is here.

What Should You Delegate First?

Start with the task you have rescheduled the most times. It is the cheapest possible test of whether delegating works for you. Not the longest one — the one you keep avoiding. It is small, it is overdue, and handing it off tells you within two weeks whether delegating works for you.

After that, go after whatever interrupts you during the hours you are supposed to be earning. That is the phone, and it is the single most expensive thing on the list, because a missed call is a lost job and you never find out which ones they were.

What you should not do is hand over everything at once and hope. The owners who get the most out of an assistant tend to give them one process, watch it get documented, and then give them the next.

If You Have Never Written the Process Down

A process that has never been written down is harder to hand to anyone, an assistant or an employee. An assistant will also do this with you — documenting the procedures as they take them on is part of the work.

Virtual Assistant or First Employee: Common Questions

Should I hire a virtual assistant or my first employee?

It depends on the work, not the stage. Work that is repeatable, can be written down, and happens at a desk is assistant work — scheduling, inbox and phone coverage, invoicing and chasing, CRM upkeep, reporting, document collection. Work that needs someone physically present, that requires a professional license, or that involves supervising other people needs a hire. If the work is none of these yet because nobody has defined it, neither one will go well until you have.

When should I hire help for my small business?

When the admin has started costing you revenue rather than time. If quotes go out late, calls get returned a day later and invoices sit unchased because you ran out of day, the work has outgrown you — regardless of your headcount or turnover. Most owners reach that point well before they can afford a full-time employee, which is why the first hire is often not a hire at all.

What if I need help but cannot afford a full-time employee?

That is the most common version of this problem, and it is what part-time exists for. A VRA assistant is $800 a month for 20 hours a week, or $1,500 for 40, against a $48,310 median salary for a full-time administrative assistant (Bureau of Labor Statistics, May 2025). You can start at twenty hours and move to forty at any time.

How do I know I have outgrown doing the admin myself?

The usual signal is not volume, it is slippage. Quotes go out late, calls get returned the next day, invoices sit unchased, and the work that grows the business only happens after hours. When the admin has started costing you revenue rather than time, it has outgrown you.

Is a virtual assistant cheaper than an employee?

On salary alone, substantially. The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the median wage for secretaries and administrative assistants at $48,310 a year as of May 2025. A full-time VRA assistant is $1,500 a month, $18,000 a year, for forty hours a week — and payroll taxes, benefits, equipment and paid time off are additions to the first number, not the second.

What should I delegate first?

The task you have rescheduled most often. It is usually the one you dislike rather than the one that takes longest, and it is the clearest test of whether delegating works for you. After that, hand over whatever interrupts you most during the hours you are supposed to be selling or building.

Can I start part-time and add coverage later?

Yes. Part-time is $800 a month for 20 hours a week and full-time is $1,500 for 40, and increased coverage is available at any time. Faster than a hire, which is usually the real comparison. Every assistant has already completed a mandatory four-week instructor-led training program before they are matched to you, so the general capability is in place on day one and what remains is your systems and your process.
