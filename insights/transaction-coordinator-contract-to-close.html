What a Transaction Coordinator Does From Contract to Close

Deals rarely fall apart over the contract. They fall apart, or arrive late, over a resale certificate nobody ordered until week three.

A transaction coordinator is the person who owns a real estate file between the signed contract and the closing table. They distribute the contract, calendar every date in it, collect and chase the documents each party owes, schedule access, keep all sides informed, assemble the broker compliance file and close it out afterwards.

The role is administrative rather than advisory. The agent advises the client and negotiates. The coordinator makes sure nothing in the file is late or missing. Here is what that looks like stage by stage.

What Happens in the First Twenty-Four Hours?

More than anything else in the file, and it sets whether the rest is calm or frantic.

  • The executed contract goes to every party the same day — co-operating agent, lender, title or escrow, and the client
  • Title or escrow is opened and the file number circulated so nobody is guessing it later
  • Earnest money and option fee delivery is confirmed and the receipt documented
  • Every date in the contract is calendared, with internal reminders set ahead of each
  • An introduction email goes out so each party knows who to ask for what
  • Third-party documents with long lead times are ordered now, not when they are needed

That last one is the whole game, and the reason a coordinator pays for themselves on a single file.

Which Deadlines Actually Carry Consequences?

A small number, and they are not the ones generating the most email.

The option or inspection period has the hardest edge, because the right to walk away expires on a clock and no amount of goodwill brings it back.

Financing and appraisal contingency dates come next. Missing one usually does not kill a deal, but it moves the leverage in it.

Earnest money and option fee delivery is the quiet one. It is rarely late, but when receipt was never documented it becomes an argument at exactly the wrong moment.

HOA resale certificates and surveys are the usual cause of a delayed closing rather than a dead one. They depend on a third party with no stake in your timeline.

Everything else in the file matters and is recoverable. Sorting the dates this way is what lets one person carry several files at once without carrying the anxiety of all of them.

What Documents Stall Deals Most Often?

Documents somebody else has to produce. The pattern holds across markets:

  • HOA resale certificates and association documents
  • Surveys, and the decision about whether an existing one is acceptable
  • Payoff statements from the seller’s lender
  • Death certificates and probate orders on an estate sale
  • Corporate resolutions and entity documents when an LLC or trust is on title
  • Power of attorney paperwork when a party cannot sign in person

None of these are within the agent’s control, which is exactly why they need somebody whose job is to order them early and follow up until they arrive. A document requested in week one and chased twice is rarely the reason a closing moves.

The same material comes up on the title side of the file. What a title assistant does from order to close covers it from the other desk.

What Should the Client Hear, and How Often?

A weekly update, whether or not anything happened.

This is the part agents postpone and clients resent waiting for. A buyer three weeks into a contract does not know whether silence means everything is fine or something is wrong, and will assume the second. Three lines — what happened this week, what is next, what we need from you — eliminates the most common complaint in a transaction.

It is also the easiest thing to delegate without giving up the relationship. The update goes out in the agent’s name, on a schedule, to a standard the agent set. The agent still takes the call when the client has a real question.

What Can an Agent Hand Over, and What Cannot Move?

The line is cleaner than most people expect.

Delegable: distributing the contract, calendaring dates and sending reminders, ordering and chasing third-party documents, scheduling inspections and access, routing lender requests, sending amendments for signature, filing returned documents, status updates to every party, assembling the compliance file, post-close closeout and the review request.

Not delegable: advising the client, negotiating, and any judgment about what a contract term means or what the client should do about it.

Almost everything in the first list is time-sensitive and almost nothing in it requires a license. That is the definition of work that should not be sitting on a producing agent’s desk.

Does This Work the Same on a Commercial Deal?

The shape is the same and the specifics are not. A commercial file runs from an LOI rather than a contract, due diligence replaces the option period, and the document chase involves estoppels, rent rolls and service contracts rather than HOA certificates. The coordination problem — four parties, a date that matters, a document somebody else controls — is identical.

Who Does This Work?

Three options, and the right one depends on volume. An agent does it themselves, which works until it doesn’t. A team hires a coordinator, which is a salary and a hiring process. Or the file work goes to a trained assistant.

That third route is what we do: a transaction coordinator virtual assistant, trained on the documents and timelines before placement, at a flat monthly rate rather than a charge per file.

Transaction Coordination: Common Questions

What is a transaction coordinator?

A transaction coordinator is the person who owns a real estate file between the signed contract and the closing table. They open the file and distribute the contract, calendar every deadline in it, collect and chase the documents each party owes, schedule the inspection, appraisal access and final walkthrough, keep all sides informed, assemble the broker compliance file and close the file out afterwards. The role is administrative rather than advisory: the agent advises the client and negotiates, the coordinator makes sure nothing in the file is late or missing.

What can an agent hand to a coordinator safely?

The coordination and records layer: distributing the executed contract, calendaring dates and sending reminders, ordering and chasing third-party documents, scheduling inspections and access, routing lender requests, sending amendments for signature, filing returned documents, updating every party on status, assembling the compliance file and closing it out. What stays with the agent is advising the client, negotiating, and any judgment about what a contract term means or what the client should do about it.

Does a coordinator talk to the client?

Usually yes, and it is most of the value. The status update a buyer wants — what happened this week, what is next, what you need from me — is the thing agents most often postpone and clients most often resent waiting for. A coordinator sending it on a schedule, in the agent’s voice and to the agent’s standard, removes the most common complaint in a transaction without taking the relationship away from the agent.
