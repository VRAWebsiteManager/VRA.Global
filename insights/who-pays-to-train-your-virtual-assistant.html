Who Pays to Train Your Virtual Assistant?

The short answer: somebody always pays to train a virtual assistant. The only question is whether the bill arrives as a line on an invoice or as six weeks of your own evenings. An agency that places an assistant first and trains them afterward has not removed the training cost. It has moved it onto the client, where it shows up as your time instead of their expense.

That is the cost almost nobody prices in when they compare quotes, and it is usually the largest number in the deal. Here is where it lands in each hiring model, what “already trained” is worth asking about, what you still have to teach no matter who you hire, and what happens to that investment when someone leaves.

Why Does Hiring an Assistant Cost You Weeks Before It Saves You Any?

You hire help to get time back. On day one you have less of it than you did the week before.

Nothing the assistant needs exists yet. The processes live in your head, not in a document. The logins are in four different places. Half the steps in your own workflow turn out to be decisions only you make, and you find that out the first time someone asks. So you start writing it all down, which is work you were already too busy to do. Every task you hand over has to be explained properly once before it saves you anything, and explaining it properly takes longer than doing it.

This is where most first assistants quietly fail, and it is almost never because the assistant was incapable. It is because the owner ran out of time to train them and went back to doing the work. It is easy to keep paying for someone you cannot keep busy, because showing them how to do things costs more time than you have to spend.

“I had to spend hours of my days training them, writing out SOPs. VRA came in prepared and ready to go.”
— Brandon Lara, 90-day check-in

Brandon is describing the difference between two bills for the same thing. Both assistants had to be trained. In one case he paid for it himself, in hours; in the other, it had already been paid before she arrived.

Comparing two monthly rates without asking this question compares two different purchases. One includes the training. One sends it to you later, in a currency you cannot invoice.

What Does “Already Trained” Actually Mean?

The phrase gets used loosely enough to be worth four specific questions: What does the training cover? How long is it? Who teaches it? And what happens to the applicants who do not pass it?

That last one tells you the most. A program everybody graduates from is an orientation, not a training program. The standard is what makes the credential mean anything.

At VRA, every assistant completes four weeks of mandatory, instructor-led training before being placed with a client, and most applicants never get that far. In the most recent cohort, 1,800 applications produced 16 graduating assistants — only 0.89% of our latest applicant cohort passed. The assistant you interview is one of the people who finished.

“I was really impressed with the level of training and professionalism that she started with from day one.”
— Karen Gonzalez, 30-day check-in

How Do You Train a Virtual Assistant on Your Own Business?

Your systems, your clients, your preferences and your voice. That part is irreducible, and any provider who implies otherwise is overselling. No training program can teach someone a business it has never seen.

What changes is where you start. You are showing a trained professional how your process runs, not explaining what a CRM is for. And some of the groundwork is already done before your first call: your assistant reads your DISC and KPA reports, so they know how you prefer to work and where your pain points are before you have to describe either.

“I think one of my favorite parts about having an assistant, especially through VRA, is because she knew the tools before we even started.”
— Brandon Lara, 90-day check-in

We commit to 90% proficiency in your systems within 5 business days of your assistant starting with you. In practice, clients describe the ramp in weeks rather than months.

“I really only have to give her direction, and after learning our processes and systems over those first two weeks she was able to take the tasks that I was giving her and really run with it.”
— Hannah DuBose, Keller Williams Heritage

What Happens to the Training You Paid For If the Assistant Leaves?

This is the second bill, and it is the one that catches people. If you carried the training yourself, turnover means carrying it again: documenting, explaining and correcting from the beginning, while the work piles up a second time. A replacement promise that only applies inside a trial window moves that risk back to you the moment the window closes.

At VRA you can swap in a different assistant at any point, at no extra cost, and the replacement has completed the same four weeks the first one did. You are not starting over on the fundamentals, because you never paid for them in the first place.

Does the Training Stop Once Your Assistant Is Placed?

No, and that matters more over a year than anything in the first month. Assistants keep developing after placement, and VRA checks in with every client at 14, 30, 60 and 90 days — partly to support the assistant, partly to make sure the fit is right while it is still early enough to change. Those conversations are on camera, with transcripts, at every stage.

“Jerome got to a fast start at the beginning and he continues to improve, develop, learn.”
— Ken Foster, 60-day check-in

Why Does VRA Train Before Placement Instead of After?

Because our founder was the client in the version of this story that does not work. Michael Bossart hired a virtual assistant for his own real estate business and lost weeks to training someone from scratch, until it was faster to do the work himself — which is the whole problem the assistant was supposed to solve. VRA was built so that a client never has to make that trade: the training happens first, at our cost, and the check-ins exist because people need help getting used to working with an assistant at all. The story behind the company is the argument for how it is structured.

How do you train a virtual assistant?

In two parts, and only one of them has to be yours. The first is the job itself: professional administrative practice, CRM and document work, and the tools a back office runs on. The second is your business, which means your systems, your clients and your standards. Hire direct and you do both, and the first part takes weeks before the second can even start. VRA completes the first part before placement, so onboarding is limited to the second: start with one process rather than a list, put the work somewhere you can both see it, and say something at two weeks instead of two months.

How long does it take to train a virtual assistant?

Teaching someone the fundamentals of professional administrative work takes weeks, and teaching them your particular business takes more. VRA splits those two jobs. The four weeks of instructor-led training happen before placement, on our side. What is left for you is your own systems and preferences, and we commit to 90% proficiency in your systems within 5 business days of your assistant starting with you.
