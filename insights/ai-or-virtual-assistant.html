Insights / AI or a Virtual Assistant
AI and Delegation

Should You Just Use AI Instead of Hiring a Virtual Assistant?

It is a fair question. You can open ChatGPT or Claude, describe what you need, and have a usable draft in under a minute. If that is true, why pay for a virtual assistant at all?

The honest answer is that the two solve different problems. AI gives you a tool. A virtual assistant gives you back your hours. Those sound similar until you look at where your day actually goes.

What Does AI Genuinely Do Well?

Start with what is true, because the case does not need overstating.

AI is very good at producing a first version of something. A listing description, a renewal reminder, a social post, a job ad, a summary of a long document, a draft reply to an awkward email. It does not get tired, it does not mind being asked again, and it costs very little.

If your bottleneck is a blank page, AI removes it. That is real, and any business owner who has not tried it for that is leaving something on the table.

Where Do Your Hours Actually Go?

Here is the part that gets skipped.

AI can write the renewal email. Someone still has to decide who it goes to, pull the list, send it, notice who did not reply, chase them on Thursday, update the record, and tell you what came back. The draft was two minutes. The rest was the afternoon.

That pattern repeats across much of what an owner hands off:

AI or a virtual assistant, task by task
The taskWhat AI gives youWhat still has to happen
A renewal or follow-up email A draft in the right tone, in seconds Deciding who it goes to, sending it, noticing who did not reply, and chasing them on Thursday
A list to work from A tidy format, once it has the data Pulling the data out of your CRM or management system, and spotting the client sitting in it three times under different names
Data entry A clean, paste-ready block Entering it in the system you actually use, and catching the line that does not reconcile
A document or signature packet A first draft of the wording Assembling it, sending it out for signature, and chasing whoever has not signed
Scheduling A suggested time and a polite note Booking it, confirming it, and rearranging it when the contractor moves
An inbound call A script, or an automated voice that takes a message Someone who can answer the question the caller actually has, book the job, and call Tuesday’s customer back
An outstanding quote A well-judged nudge, drafted Knowing which quotes are still open, calling the ones worth calling, and recording what the customer said

In each row the draft is the fast part. The follow-through is the slow part, and the follow-through is what was taking your hours in the first place.

Using AI yourself also costs more time than people count. You have to know what to ask for, read what comes back, catch where it is wrong, and ask again. That is a skill, and it is a real one, but it is a skill you are exercising in hours you said you did not have.

The Second Gap: AI Does What You Ask

There is a more interesting difference, and it is harder to see until you have lived it.

AI answers the question you put to it. It does not notice the question you forgot.

An assistant who has been inside your business for sixty days knows that the renewal list has three clients on it who need chasing twice, that one vendor sends invoices to the wrong address, and that the file you are about to send is missing a page. Nobody asked them to track that. They noticed.

A title client described it this way at her sixty-day check-in:

“She is a self-starter, and she’s finding things that we didn’t expect her really to find.”
— Debbie Yates, President & Escrow Officer, Key Title of Texas

Another client, after sixty days:

“She’s taken a lot of notes, recorded every Zoom meeting that we’ve had, so any time she does have questions she always refers back to the notes or the videos first.”
— Hannah DuBose, Marketing Team, Keller Williams Heritage

Neither of those is a prompt you could have written.

What It Looks Like in Your Business

The split shows up differently depending on what you do.

  • Real estate. AI drafts the listing description. Your assistant uploads it, updates the CRM, confirms the photographer, sends the disclosure packet, and chases the signature that is holding up the file.
  • Title and escrow. AI can summarize a document. Your assistant orders the title search, requests the payoff statement, follows up on the HOA demand that is running late, and keeps the closing file organized while the examiner does the work only the examiner can do.
  • Insurance agencies. AI can write the client reminder. Your assistant pulls the renewals from the agency management system, sends the reminders, collects the updated exposure information, prepares the certificates from existing policy data, and routes anything about coverage to your licensed staff.
  • Home services and the trades. AI can write the estimate follow-up. Your assistant answers the phone while the crews are in the field, books the job, and calls the customer back about the one from Tuesday.

In none of those is AI the thing standing between you and your evening. The coordination is.

What Does AI Get Wrong, and Who Answers for It?

AI is fluent whether or not it is right. It will give you a renewal date, a commission split or a contract deadline in the same even tone whether it read the document correctly or filled the gap with something plausible, and the mistakes do not announce themselves. That matters most in exactly the work where being wrong is expensive: dates, figures, names on a document, anything a client is going to act on. Someone has to check it against the source, and that someone is either you or a person you are paying.

There is a quieter version of this, and it is the one owners actually run into. A workflow you set up months ago and stopped thinking about — the form that routes new inquiries, the sync between two systems, the sequence that follows up after a showing — breaks without announcing it. Automation does not fail loudly. It stops, and because it was built to run without you, nobody is watching the place where it stopped. You find out when a client asks why nobody got back to them.

The harder version of the same problem is a system that is still running and no longer doing what it was built to do. Nothing is broken, the reports still generate, and the thing it was supposed to accomplish for the person at the other end quietly stopped happening. Uptime is not the test. Someone has to be close enough to the work to notice that the inquiries are not being answered, or that a number has not moved in three weeks, and to know that is wrong. The more of your operation runs unattended, the more of it can be quietly not running.

Then there is the question of what goes into the prompt in the first place. Client files, contracts, policy details and transaction records are not yours to paste wherever is convenient, and the obligations you work under do not relax because a tool is useful. Deciding what may be handed to which tool is a judgment call about your own business, and it is better made deliberately than by whoever is in a hurry.

And when something does go wrong, accountability does not divide. A tool cannot be answerable to your client, because it has no stake in the consequence. The practical difference when a task sits with a person is that one named individual owns the outcome, is reachable when it slips, and carries what happened into the next one. None of this is an argument against using AI. It is the reason the tool and the person are not substitutes for each other.

Why Is Human Attention Getting More Valuable, Not Less?

The assumption underneath most of this debate is that as AI gets better, the human part of a service matters less. The market is moving the other way.

Luxury hospitality is where it shows first, because that is where people pay closest attention to how they are treated. As check-in, booking and routine service questions move to software, the premium has shifted to the things software cannot do: the restaurant where someone remembers your table, the hotel manager who knows your name, the tailor who insists on another fitting. Robb Report recently called human interaction the ultimate luxury good, and the hospitality trade press has spent the year on the same argument — Rome Future Week opened on “humans as luxury”, and Four Seasons has made the same case about personalization and the human touch.

The same shift is underway in ordinary businesses, with the price pointing in the opposite direction. Your clients are now getting automated replies from most of the businesses they deal with. Against that background, the follow-up call where someone already knows the file, or the message that answers the question actually asked rather than the one a template assumed, stops being ordinary and starts being the thing they remember about working with you.

That is the part worth protecting, and it is the first part to erode when the owner is the only person available to do it. The hours go to the volume, and the attention that distinguishes the business gets whatever is left at the end of the day.

Which is the useful way to read the trend. Human attention is becoming scarce and expensive at the top of the market. Handed to a trained assistant on a flat monthly rate, it is neither.

The Part Nobody Says Out Loud

Your problem probably isn’t output. It’s the number of things sitting on your own plate.

You built the business on something you are good at — selling, closing, cooking, fixing, advising. The admin came with it. It is not what you trained for, it is not what you enjoy, and it is not what the business pays you to do. AI makes that work faster to start. It does not take it off you.

And there is a reason the obvious fix has not happened. Hiring is a salary, plus payroll taxes, benefits and equipment, carried whether the quarter is good or not. If the work is clearly there and the money for another salary isn’t yet, the work stays where it is, and the things that would grow the business — the marketing, the follow-up on last month’s leads, the process nobody has written down — wait behind the things that keep today running.

That gap is not solved by a better drafting tool. It is solved by capacity. We walk through the salary comparison in more detail in a virtual assistant or your first employee.

What Should You Hand to AI, and What Needs a Person?

A practical way to split it:

  • Give AI the blank page. First drafts, summaries, rewrites, brainstorms, formatting, turning notes into something readable. Anything where the work is producing text and you are the one who will act on it.
  • Give an assistant the loop. Anything that has to be sent, tracked, entered, chased, confirmed, filed or followed up — work that lives in your systems and ends with something having actually happened. That is the part that was eating your week.

You can use both, which is the point. The question is not which one to pick. It is which parts of your week you want back.

AI and Virtual Assistants: Common Questions

Does AI still need human oversight?

Yes, and more of it as more of the business runs on its own. Two failures need a person. AI is fluent whether or not it is right, so dates, figures and anything a client will act on have to be checked against the source. And automation that was set up correctly months ago can stop without announcing it, or keep running while no longer accomplishing what it was built to accomplish — which nobody catches unless somebody is close enough to the work to notice. Oversight is not watching the tool. It is watching whether the system is still doing its job for the person it was set up to help.

As AI improves, does a human assistant become less valuable?

The opposite is happening in the places that watch it most closely. As automation absorbs routine contact, the human part of a service becomes the part clients notice and the part they will pay a premium for — luxury hospitality has spent the year making that argument about itself. The work AI takes over is largely the work nobody remembers receiving. What rises in value is judgment on the things that are expensive to get wrong, and one named person who is answerable for the outcome.

Can AI replace a virtual assistant?

Not for the follow-through, which is what an assistant actually does. AI produces drafts and summaries quickly. A virtual assistant takes the whole task — sending, tracking, entering, chasing, filing and following up inside the systems your business already uses. The draft is the fast part of the work; the follow-through is the part that takes the hours.

If I already use ChatGPT, do I still need a virtual assistant?

It depends what is slowing you down. If your bottleneck is producing text, AI may be enough on its own. If your bottleneck is that things are not getting sent, chased, entered or followed up, that is work that has to be done in your systems by someone accountable for it, and AI leaves that with you.

Do VRA assistants use AI?

Yes. Claude and ChatGPT are part of the Essential Toolkit each assistant completes before their first client interview, and there is further AI training available for assistants whose clients need it.

Isn’t a virtual assistant more expensive than an AI subscription?

In pure subscription terms, yes. The comparison that matters is against your own hours, or against a salary with payroll taxes and benefits on top. A part-time assistant is $800 a month for 20 hours a week at one flat rate, which is why part-time exists for businesses where the work is there before another salary is.

What are the risks of using AI instead of a virtual assistant?

AI is fluent whether or not it is right, so a wrong date or figure arrives in the same even tone as a correct one, and anything a client is going to act on has to be checked against the source. There is also the question of which client information goes into a prompt at all, which is a judgment call about the obligations your business works under. And accountability does not divide: a tool cannot be answerable to your client, so the checking and the owning stay with a person either way.

What can an assistant do that AI cannot?

Act in your systems, carry a task to completion, and notice what nobody asked them to look for. AI answers the question you put to it. An assistant who knows your business raises the one you forgot.
