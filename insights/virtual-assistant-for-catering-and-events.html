Virtual Assistant for Catering and Private Events: Covering the Booking Desk

The short answer: a catering virtual assistant runs the desk around your events — the inquiry inbox, the proposal, the tasting, the contract and deposit, the event sheet, the supplier confirmations, the final count, the invoice and the follow-up that turns one event into next year’s. The menus, the pricing, the food and the decisions stay with you. This guide walks the booking from first inquiry to final payment and shows where an assistant takes over at each stage.

How Is Catering Admin Different From Restaurant Admin?

Restaurant admin arrives in pieces through the day and can usually wait an hour. Catering admin arrives with a date attached, and the date is the whole thing. An inquiry for a wedding in June is worth something on Tuesday and worth nothing in three weeks, because by then the couple has booked someone who answered.

Two other things make it harder than it looks. Every booking is its own small project with its own paperwork, so ten events in a month is ten sets of counts, dietary notes, rentals and load-in times rather than one list ten times longer. And the admin peaks at precisely the moment the kitchen is least able to absorb it: the season that fills your calendar is the season that fills your days.

Which is why the catering desk is usually the first thing an owner means to get to and the last thing they do. The day-to-day version of this problem is covered in the restaurant admin you can hand off, and what to keep.

From Inquiry to Deposit: Where Bookings Are Won and Lost

This is the stretch where money is made or quietly lost, and almost all of it is desk work:

  • Watching the inquiry inbox, the web form and the social messages, and replying while the inquiry is still warm
  • Capturing the details that decide everything else — date, headcount, venue, service style, budget range, dietary requirements
  • Checking the date against the calendar and flagging a clash before anyone promises anything
  • Building the proposal from the menus and the pricing you have already set, and sending it once you have approved it
  • Following up on the proposal on a schedule rather than when someone remembers
  • Booking tastings, confirming them, and reminding the client the day before
  • Issuing the contract, chasing the signature, chasing the deposit, and logging all of it where you can see it

None of that requires a decision about food or price. All of it requires somebody whose day is not interrupted by a delivery or a walk-in.

Which Event Paperwork Can You Hand Off?

Every booked event generates a document set, and the catering business runs on how accurately it is kept: the event order or run sheet, the guest count and the deadline for the final count, allergen and dietary notes, the rental and equipment list, venue contacts and load-in windows, the staffing roster, and the supplier orders that follow from all of it.

This is the part that punishes a missed detail rather than a missed hour. A final count logged a day late, an allergen note that did not make it onto the sheet, a load-in window nobody confirmed with the venue — each one is small on paper and expensive on the day. An assistant whose job is the paperwork keeps it in one place and current, and chases the pieces that have not come back.

The Week Of

In the last seven days before an event the work turns into confirmation: the final headcount chased and logged, suppliers confirmed and substitutions sorted, the staff roster circulated and availability checked, the venue called about setup and access, the prep and packing lists distributed, and every late change written somewhere the whole team can see it rather than living in one person’s phone.

What that adds up to is the people on site arriving knowing exactly what was agreed, without anyone having to reconstruct it on the morning.

After the Event: The Part That Pays for Itself

Catering margin lives in repeat business and referrals, and the follow-up is the first thing to go when the next event is already loading in. Handing it over is the cheapest win on this list:

  • The final invoice, sent the same week, and the balance chased until it lands
  • A thank-you and a feedback request while the event is still fresh
  • A review request, and the review answered when it arrives
  • A short post-event note on what to repeat and what to change next time
  • The client added to a list that gets contacted before the same season comes round again
  • Photographs and social content prepared for your approval rather than never posted

Questions Catering and Event Businesses Ask

What does a virtual assistant do for a catering business?

The booking desk, end to end: watching the inquiry inbox and web forms, capturing event details, drafting proposals from your menus and your pricing, booking tastings, issuing contracts and chasing deposits, building the event sheet, confirming suppliers and final counts the week of, then invoicing, requesting the review and following up for repeat business afterwards. The cooking, the costing and the decisions stay with you.

Can an assistant write catering proposals and quotes?

They can build them, not price them. Your assistant assembles the proposal from the menus and the pricing you have already set, fills in the event details the client gave, and sends it once you have approved it. What a job should cost, and whether to take it at all, stays with you.

Can an assistant chase deposits and final payments?

Yes, and it is one of the first things worth handing over. Deposit reminders, contract signatures, final balance chasing and payment tracking are procedural work with dates attached, which is exactly the work that slips when the kitchen is busy. The assistant follows up on the schedule you set; disputes and anything that needs a decision come back to you.

Will an assistant work in Caterease, Tripleseat or HoneyBook?

They work in whatever you already run. VRA assistants are trained on the category rather than one vendor’s product, so Caterease, Tripleseat, HoneyBook, 17hats, Better Cater, a shared calendar or a spreadsheet are all the same kind of problem. We commit to 90% proficiency in your systems within 5 business days of starting with you, working inside your own accounts on access you grant and can revoke.

Our events are seasonal. Does a virtual assistant still make sense?

That is what part-time is for. A part-time assistant is $800 a month for 20 hours a week and a full-time assistant is $1,500 a month for 40, and you can move between them when your season turns rather than waiting for a renewal date. The work that matters in a quiet month — following up on last season’s clients, keeping the enquiry list warm — is the work that fills the busy one.

Can one assistant cover both the restaurant and the catering side?

Often, yes. The two desks share systems and suppliers, and an assistant who already knows your reservation book and your vendor list is the natural person to run the event inbox too. Where the catering side grows into its own pipeline, a second assistant takes it rather than splitting one person across both.
