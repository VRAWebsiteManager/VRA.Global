Mortgage & Lending

Loan Officer Assistant or Virtual Assistant?

The question usually arrives at the worst moment: the pipeline is full, the inbox is three days deep, and you are about to post a job you have not fully thought through because something has to give.

Loan officers and mortgage brokers ask it as a choice between two hires, and it is not one. A loan officer assistant and a virtual assistant are not competing for the same seat, because the job a loan officer assistant does is really two jobs stapled together, and only one of them can be done by someone without a license.

Once you separate the halves, the question stops being which assistant and becomes which half am I paying a salary for. That is a more useful question, and for most offices it has a cheaper answer than the one they were about to choose.

The Job Was Always Two Jobs

Look at any loan officer assistant job posting and you will find two lists wearing one title. One list is regulated work that touches the application and the borrower’s financial details. The other is the volume of coordination that surrounds it — the emails, the scheduling, the referral partners, the CRM, the status updates nobody has time to send.

The second list is where the hours actually go. It is also the list that does not require anyone to be licensed, sit in your office, or be on your payroll. Most offices never separate the two, so they hire one person for both and pay a salary that was priced for the regulated half.

Already know which half you want covered? The mortgage virtual assistant page sets out what an assistant takes on in a lending office.

What Stays With Your Licensed Staff?

VRA states this line the same way on every lending engagement, and it is worth repeating here because it is the part people get wrong: no taking applications, no collecting income, asset or credit details, no access to the loan origination system, and no underwriting or condition-clearing work.

That is not a limitation VRA invented to be cautious. It is the shape of the role, and an assistant who is vague about it is a liability rather than a help. Every VRA mortgage assistant completes a four-module program that includes compliance boundaries for unlicensed staff, so the line is trained rather than assumed.

What the Other Half Looks Like

Strip out the regulated work and what remains is substantial, repetitive, and almost entirely desk-based:

  • Inbox coverage — triage, replies to borrowers and referral partners, nothing falling through
  • Pipeline tracking and status updates, so borrowers hear from the office before they chase it
  • Appointment scheduling and calendar management
  • Referral partner communication, the agent relationships that generate the next loan
  • CRM hygiene — the part everyone intends to do and nobody does
  • Marketing: social content, email campaigns, review requests
  • Invoicing, reporting and document organization

None of that needs a license. All of it needs doing, and most of it is what a loan officer ends up doing at nine at night.

What Does Each One Cost?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the median wage for loan interviewers and clerks at $50,020 a year as of May 2025. That is salary alone — before payroll taxes, benefits, equipment, software seats, or the cost of recruiting and replacing someone.

A VRA assistant is $1,500 a month full-time at 40 hours a week, or $800 a month part-time at 20. Full-time works out to $18,000 a year, and the monthly figure is the whole figure: no payroll taxes, no benefits, no equipment, no recruiting fee underneath it.

What sits inside a monthly rate is covered separately. That gap is not a claim that one replaces the other. It is the cost of the half you can delegate, set against the cost of hiring someone for both halves. If the regulated work genuinely needs a person of its own, you still need that person. The question is whether the coordination work needs to be on the same payroll line.

When Do You Actually Need the Hire?

There are offices — broker shops and retail lending offices alike — where a licensed assistant is the right answer and no amount of delegation substitutes for it. If the bottleneck is applications waiting to be taken, income and asset documentation waiting to be collected, or conditions waiting to be cleared, that is licensed work and it needs licensed staff. Handing it to an unlicensed assistant is not a saving; it is an exposure.

The more common situation is an office where the licensed work fits the people who already do it, and everything around it has quietly become a second full-time job. That office does not need a second licensed hire. It needs the second job to stop landing on the first.

What One Loan Officer Handed Over

James Del Bosque at GFS Home Loans was asked at his 90-day check-in what the biggest task was that he had given his assistant. It was not anything near the file.

“He has been able to take ownership of an inbox for me, and he has been able to keep on top of that inbox and respond back to clients and referral partners.”

Asked whether things were still slipping, he put the problem the way most loan officers would recognize it:

“Keeping on top of emails is really important, and that’s part of the business. When we get busy, a lot of things seem to fall between the cracks because we’re doing multiple things and multiple tasks.”

Inbox ownership is not glamorous and it is not regulated. It is also the thing that was costing him loans, because the borrower who does not hear back and the referral partner who does not get an answer are both expensive in a way that never shows up on a report. You can watch the full check-in, transcript included.

So Which One Should You Hire?

Split the role before you hire for it. Write down what your assistant would do in a week, then mark every line that requires a license. If the licensed column is the long one, hire. If the unlicensed column is the long one — and in most lending offices it is — you are about to pay a salary to get your inbox covered.

If you want help drawing that line against your own week, the Needs Assessment takes about two minutes, or you can take the loan officer delegation guide and do it yourself. If you already know you want the unlicensed half covered, what to hand off, and in what order is the next piece.

Loan Officer Assistant vs Virtual Assistant: Common Questions

What is the difference between a loan officer assistant and a virtual assistant?

A loan officer assistant is usually one employee doing two different jobs: regulated work that touches the application and the borrower’s financial details, and the coordination work around it. A virtual assistant does the second half only, remotely and without being on payroll. They are not substitutes for each other, because only one of them can do the licensed half.

Can a virtual assistant take loan applications?

No. A VRA assistant does not take applications, does not collect income, asset or credit details, does not have access to the loan origination system, and does not do underwriting or condition-clearing work. That work stays with your licensed staff.

Can a mortgage virtual assistant work in my loan origination system?

No. LOS access sits on the licensed side of the line and stays with your own staff. An assistant works in the systems around it — your inbox, your calendar, your CRM, your marketing tools — on access you provision and can revoke at any time.

How much does a loan officer assistant cost compared to a virtual assistant?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the median wage for loan interviewers and clerks at $50,020 a year as of May 2025, before payroll taxes, benefits, equipment and recruiting. A VRA assistant is $1,500 a month full-time at 40 hours a week, or $800 a month part-time at 20 — $18,000 a year full-time, with the monthly figure being the whole figure. The comparison is not like for like, because the salaried role includes licensed work the assistant does not do.

Do I still need a licensed assistant if I hire a virtual assistant?

If the bottleneck is applications waiting to be taken, income and asset documentation waiting to be collected, or conditions waiting to be cleared, then yes — that is licensed work and no amount of delegation substitutes for a licensed person. The more common situation is an office where the licensed work fits the people already doing it and the coordination around it has quietly become a second full-time job. That office does not need a second licensed hire.

Can one virtual assistant support more than one loan officer?

Yes. A full-time assistant works for one client exclusively, and within that office they can support more than one loan officer. How the hours are divided is a scheduling conversation rather than a licensing one, and it is worth having before the assistant starts rather than after.

Should I hire a loan officer assistant or a virtual assistant first?

Write down what the role would do in a week, then mark every line that requires a license. If the licensed column is the long one, hire. If the unlicensed column is the long one, which it is in most lending offices, you would be paying a salary largely to get coordination work covered.

Do mortgage virtual assistants get compliance training?

Every VRA mortgage assistant completes a four-module program that includes compliance boundaries for unlicensed staff, on top of the mandatory four-week training every VRA assistant completes before client work. The point is that the line is trained rather than left to judgment.
