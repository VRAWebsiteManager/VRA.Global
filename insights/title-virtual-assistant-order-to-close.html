Title & Escrow

What a Title and Escrow Assistant Does, From Order Opening to Recording

The short answer: a title and escrow assistant works the file, stage by stage — opening the order, ordering the search and logging what comes back, tracking the outstanding conditions and chasing the documents that clear them, keeping the agent, the lender and the underwriter current, assembling the closing package, booking the signing, then recording, the final policy and the post-closing checklist. The examination, the commitment decision and anything touching the escrow account stay with your licensed staff. This walks the file from order to recording and shows where an assistant picks up each stage.

Opening the Order and Building the File

The first hour of a file sets up every problem you will have later. Names spelled from the contract rather than from the vesting deed. A legal description pulled short. A lender contact nobody wrote down, so three people email the wrong person for two weeks. Payoff requests that go out on day nine instead of day one.

An assistant opens the order the same way each time: the file set up in your production system, parties and contacts entered from the contract and the lender’s instructions, the earnest money receipt logged, the commitment and closing dates on the calendar, and the first round of requests — payoffs, HOA, survey, tax certificates — sent the day the order opens rather than the day someone notices.

None of that is judgment work. All of it is the kind of work that gets done late when a closer is carrying thirty files.

Ordering the Search, Tracking the Exam

Your assistant orders the search, follows it until it lands, and logs what comes back against the file: the chain, the vesting, the open mortgages and liens, the judgments, the taxes, the easements and restrictions. Where an abstractor or search vendor has gone quiet, your assistant is the one asking again.

What the assistant is not doing is examining. Reading the search and deciding what the commitment will say is the examiner’s work, and it requires the judgment and the authority your examiner has. The assistant’s job is that the examiner has a complete file in front of them on the day they expected it, and that nothing in the search is sitting unlogged.

Curative: Where the Closing Date Is Actually Won or Lost

This is the stage worth paying attention to, because it is the one that quietly decides whether you close on time. A file does not usually blow a closing date because the work was hard. It blows it because an outstanding condition sat for eleven days with nobody asking about it — a payoff letter that was never acknowledged, a release the lender has not sent, an estate document waiting on an attorney, a name discrepancy nobody raised, an old lien that needs a tax-sale affidavit.

Worked properly, curative is a list with dates on it. Each outstanding item gets an owner, a request date and a follow-up date. The request goes out, the response gets logged, the follow-up goes out when the date passes, and the item gets raised when it stops moving. The number this produces — the average age of your outstanding conditions — is the single most useful figure in a title operation, and almost nobody tracks it, because tracking it is exactly the work that gets dropped first.

Judging whether a document actually clears a condition is your examiner’s call. Making sure somebody is still asking, nine days in, is the part an assistant does better than a closer with thirty files, because for the assistant it is not the thing competing with everything else.

One seasoned closer put the effect this way, sixty days in:

“She is a self-starter, and she’s finding things that we didn’t expect her really to find.”
— Debbie Yates, closer

The Updates Everyone Is Waiting For

A title operation gets judged less on the work than on whether anyone can find out where the file stands. Agents call because they cannot tell. Lenders email the same question three ways. Underwriters wait on a response to a condition that was cleared last Tuesday but never written up.

An assistant runs the status layer: a standing update to the agent and the lender at the points you decide, answers to the where-are-we emails pulled from the file rather than from memory, the production system kept current so the answer is the same whoever looks, and the underwriter correspondence organized so a response is not reconstructed from an inbox. A closer who stops fielding status questions gets back a surprising number of hours, and they are the hours that were being taken in two-minute pieces.

The Closing Package and the Signing

Assembling the package is checklist work with real consequences: the lender’s documents against the lender’s instructions, the settlement statement draft circulated for approval, the signing time confirmed with everyone who has to be in the room, the notary or signing agent booked, the confirmations and pre-closing documents out, and the whole set re-sent when a date moves — which it does.

Your assistant handles that sequence and the rebooking when it changes. Where your office uses a national signing service, the assistant coordinates with it; verifying the signing professional stays with whoever your procedures put it on. Figures on the settlement statement, approval of the statement, and the disbursement itself belong to your escrow officer. Where a reviewer wants that in writing, how a remote assistant maps to the ALTA Best Practices pillars sets out the access model in full.

After the Signing: Recording, Policy and Post-Closing

Post-closing is where files go to be forgotten. The recording confirmation nobody chased. The final policy that was never issued. The checklist half worked. The county that has gone quiet for three weeks.

This is steady, unglamorous, deadline-bearing work, which makes it the stage where an assistant turns up in your numbers fastest: recording confirmed and the recording information logged, the county followed up, the final policy package assembled, the post-closing checklist worked, the file closed out in your system, and the documents filed where your retention policy says they go.

Which Files Does This Work On?

The sequence is the same shape on a residential purchase, a refinance, a cash or investor deal and a commercial file; what changes is how much of it there is. Commercial files carry more entity documents and more parties to chase. Investor files come in volume and in batches. Refinances are lighter on parties and heavier on payoffs. Curative-heavy files are where the condition tracking above stops being administrative housekeeping and starts being the reason the file closes at all.

What Should You Watch in the First 60 Days?

Pick figures you already argue about in staff meetings, and watch them rather than the assistant:

  • Average days from order to close — slow to move, but it is the one leadership asks about
  • Share of files closing on the scheduled date — the number your referral sources actually feel
  • Files per closer — capacity, which is usually the reason you were hiring
  • Average age of outstanding conditions — moves first and predicts the other four
  • Status response time — how long an agent or lender waits for an answer

VRA checks in at 14, 30, 60 and 90 days, and those are a reasonable place to put the same five numbers on the table together.

Questions Title and Escrow Teams Ask

How does a title assistant work a file from order to close?

In stages, and the same way each time. Opening the order and building the file from the contract and the lender’s instructions. Ordering the search and logging what comes back against the commitment. Tracking the outstanding conditions and chasing the documents that clear them. Keeping the agent, the lender and the underwriter current without being asked. Assembling the closing package and booking the signing and the notary. Then recording confirmation, the final policy, the post-closing checklist and the file’s own tidy-up. What the assistant does not do is make the calls that belong to a licensed examiner or escrow officer — they move the file toward those calls and keep the record of what happened.

How does an assistant keep a curative file from going quiet?

By working the conditions as a list with dates on it rather than as a pile. Each outstanding item gets an owner, a request date and a follow-up date, so a payoff letter nobody acknowledged or an estate document sitting with an attorney surfaces on day three instead of the week of closing. The assistant sends the request, logs the response, re-sends when the date passes and raises the item when it stops moving. Deciding whether a document clears a condition is the examiner’s call; making sure somebody is still asking is the part that usually goes missing.

Can an assistant schedule signings and coordinate notaries?

Yes, and it is one of the first things a closer hands over. Confirming the time with the parties, booking the notary or signing agent and sending the confirmations, re-sending the pre-closing documents when a date moves, and following up on the funding and recording timeline afterwards. Where your office relies on a national signing service, the assistant coordinates with it rather than replacing your own verification of the signing professional.

Can an assistant handle post-closing and recording follow-up?

Yes. Confirming documents were recorded and logging the recording information, chasing a county that has gone quiet, assembling the final policy package, working the post-closing checklist, and closing the file out in your production system. Post-closing is steady, unglamorous and almost always behind, which makes it the stage where an assistant shows up in your numbers fastest.

Does the assistant touch the escrow or trust account?

No. VRA assistants have no signing and no disbursing authority, and they do not release wires. Access and permissions are determined by you and are subject to your own security, confidentiality, and compliance procedures, so what an assistant can see in your production or accounting system is what you provision and is revocable at any time. Each assistant is identity verified, is covered under VRA’s insurance, and we will have them sign an NDA whenever it is requested. If your reviewer asks who the individual is, it is one named person assigned to your office rather than a rotating pool.

What should we measure in the first 60 days?

Pick the numbers you already argue about and watch them: average days from order to close, the share of files that close on the scheduled date, files per closer, the average age of outstanding conditions, and how long an agent or lender waits for a status answer. Condition age is the most useful of the five, because it moves first and it is the one that predicts the others. VRA checks in at 14, 30, 60 and 90 days, and those check-ins are a reasonable place to look at the same figures together.
