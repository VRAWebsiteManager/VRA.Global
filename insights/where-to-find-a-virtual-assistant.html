Where to Find a Virtual Assistant, and What Each Route Costs You

Most advice on how to find a virtual assistant is a list of websites. That is the easy half of the question. The harder half is that each place you could look hands you a different amount of work to do yourself, and the rate you see quoted has almost nothing to do with what the arrangement will actually cost you.

There are five realistic routes. Here is what each one is good at, and what it quietly puts back on your plate.

Where Do People Actually Look?

  • Freelance marketplaces — browse profiles and hire direct, usually by the hour or the task.
  • Job boards — post the role yourself and run your own recruiting process.
  • Referrals — ask another owner who they use.
  • Staffing firms — a company sources candidates and places one with you.
  • VA companies — a company screens, trains and matches, then supports the placement.

The first four are ways of finding a person. The fifth is a way of buying a result. That distinction is the whole article.

There is a sixth option people weigh before any of these, which is not hiring anybody at all and handing the work to AI instead. That is a real decision with a real answer, and we have written about it separately: should you just use AI instead of hiring a virtual assistant?

What Does a Freelance Marketplace Get You?

Access to a very large pool, immediately, at the lowest published rates of any route. For a narrow, self-contained task — clean up this spreadsheet, format these fifty listings — it is hard to beat.

What it is not good at is handing over a recurring part of your week. You write the posting, read the profiles, decide which claims to believe, run a trial task, and discover what you actually hired after you have hired it. Every one of those steps is your time. Then the training is yours too, and if the first person does not work out, you start again from the posting.

Owners usually arrive at a marketplace because the rate looks like the cheapest option. It often is, measured in dollars per hour. Measured in the owner’s hours, it is frequently the most expensive thing on this list.

What About Hiring Direct From a Job Board?

The most control and the lowest ongoing rate, for the most work up front. You are running a full recruiting process: writing the role, sourcing, screening, interviewing, checking references, drafting a contract, and sorting out how you are going to pay somebody who may not be in your country. Then you train them.

This route makes sense when you know exactly what the role is, you have hired before, and you have the weeks. It goes badly when the reason you are hiring is that you have no weeks, which is usually the reason.

If you are weighing this against bringing somebody on properly, we have written separately about a virtual assistant versus your first employee.

Are Referrals Better?

They are the highest-trust route and the smallest pool. Somebody you respect tells you their assistant changed their business, and that is worth more than any profile page.

Two things limit it. The assistant who transformed that business is often at capacity. And “good for them” is not the same as “good for you” — different systems, different work, different standards. A referral is a strong signal about a provider. It is a weaker signal about a specific person being right for your desk.

Worth taking seriously, in other words, but it rarely answers the question on its own.

What Does a Staffing Firm Do Differently?

It takes the sourcing off you. The firm advertises, collects applicants, does some level of screening, and presents you with somebody to hire. That is real work removed from your plate, and for a role you can describe precisely it can be enough.

The question to ask is what happens after the placement. Sourcing and training are different services, and a firm that is excellent at the first does not necessarily do the second at all. If the answer is that the assistant arrives screened but untrained, then you have bought a shorter search and kept the training.

What Does a VA Company Do?

A VA company runs the whole chain: recruiting, screening, training, matching you to an assistant, and supporting the placement afterwards. You are not buying access to a pool. You are buying somebody who can start work.

The range of quality inside this category is wider than in any of the others, because “trained” is not a regulated word. It can mean a four-week instructor-led program. It can also mean a slide deck and a welcome call. Every company in the category uses the same vocabulary, which is why the next question matters more than the label.

What Is the Question Underneath All Five?

Who trains the assistant, and when?

That cost never disappears. It only moves. Either a provider absorbs it before the assistant reaches you, or you absorb it afterwards, in exactly the hours you were trying to free up. Comparing an hourly rate across these five routes without asking that question compares numbers that are not measuring the same thing.

How Long Until the Work Comes Off Your Plate?

This is the number worth comparing, rather than the rate.

  • Marketplace — an afternoon to browse, then weeks of screening, trialling and training before the work is genuinely gone.
  • Job board — the longest of the five. You are running recruiting from scratch before training even starts.
  • Referral — fast when the person is available, back to the other routes when they are not.
  • Staffing firm — a shorter search, with the training still ahead of you.
  • VA company — the search is the short part, if the training happened before placement.

Finding a Virtual Assistant: Common Questions

Where is the best place to find a virtual assistant?

There are five realistic places to look: freelance marketplaces, job boards where you hire direct, referrals from other owners, staffing firms that source and place, and VA companies that screen and train before placement. None is best in the abstract. The one that fits depends on whether you have time to train somebody yourself. Marketplaces and direct hiring give you the lowest rate and hand you the recruiting, screening and training. A VA company costs more per hour and does that work before the assistant reaches you.

How long does it take to find a virtual assistant?

Browsing a marketplace takes an afternoon, but screening candidates, running a trial task and training the person you pick is usually several weeks before the work genuinely comes off your plate. Hiring direct from a job board takes longest, because you are running a recruiting process from scratch. With a VA company the search itself is the short part: at VRA, once you select your assistant they can start within 24 hours of selection, because the training already happened.

Can I just hire a virtual assistant on a freelance marketplace?

Yes, and plenty of owners do. What you are buying there is access to a pool, not a vetted match. You write the posting, read the profiles, run the trial, and find out what you actually hired after you have hired it. The rate is the lowest of any route and the time cost is the highest, so it works best when the task is narrow and self-contained and badly when you need somebody to take over a recurring part of your week.

Do I have to train a virtual assistant myself?

That depends entirely on where you found them. The training does not disappear from the equation; it only moves. Either the provider does it before placement or you do it afterwards, in hours you were trying to free up in the first place. It is worth asking any provider what training happens before placement and how long it runs. VRA assistants complete a mandatory four-week instructor-led program before they are placed with anyone.

What should I ask before I commit to a VA company?

Six things: what training happens before placement and how long it runs, how many applicants actually make it through, who supervises the assistant day to day, what happens if the fit is wrong, what the commitment is and what changes at the end of it, and whether the published price is the real price once setup, overage and after-hours rates are counted. Our guide to hiring a virtual assistant goes through each one and what a good answer sounds like.

What happens if the first assistant is not the right fit?

Ask about this before you sign anything, because the answer varies more than the marketing does. Some providers offer a trial window and charge to swap once it closes. At VRA you can swap in a different assistant at any point, at no extra cost, with no trial window and no fine print, and support continues with check-ins at 14, 30, 60 and 90 days.

How much does a virtual assistant cost?

It varies by route more than by the work itself, which is why comparing hourly rates across routes is misleading. VRA is flat: $1,500 a month for a full-time assistant at 40 hours a week, or $800 a month part-time at 20 hours, with no setup or onboarding fees.
