Remote Title Assistants and ALTA Best Practices: What Your Reviewer Will Ask

The short answer: nothing in the ALTA Best Practices framework prohibits a title agency from using a remote assistant. What the framework does is hand the agency two questions — what access are you granting, and what can you document about the person you granted it to. This sets out where a remote assistant actually lands in the pillars, what we can put in your vendor file, and the one thing we cannot give you. It describes the framework as published and what VRA can document; assessing your own procedures against it is yours and your underwriter’s call, and this is not compliance advice.

Where Does a Remote Assistant Land in the ALTA Pillars?

First, a correction worth having, because it gets repeated often enough to end up in a vendor questionnaire: vendor management is not one of the seven pillars. The seven are Licensing, Escrow Trust Accounting, Protecting NPI, Settlement Processes, Title Opinions, Insurance Coverage and Consumer Complaints. Third-party service providers sit inside Pillar 3, Protecting NPI.

Which means a remote assistant touches the framework in exactly two places. Pillar 3, because they have access to non-public personal information and to your systems. And Pillar 2, Escrow Trust Accounting, because of what they must not be able to do. Everything else — licensing, title opinions, premium remittance, complaint procedures — stays where it already was.

Pillar 3: Your Plan Governs the Access You Grant

Pillar 3 asks you to maintain a written information security program. The 2023 revisions, version 4.0, tightened the parts that bear on outsourcing specifically: screening expectations were extended to anyone with access to NPI or to information systems, service providers named among them, and service provider policies are asked to be consistent with your own program. Third-party software, automated processes, APIs and add-ins are held to the same standard.

Read plainly, that puts the burden where it belongs. Your plan is the standard. A provider does not satisfy Pillar 3 on your behalf by having good intentions or a signed agreement, and no vendor can hand you compliance as a product. What a provider can do is be precise about what it is, so the comparison against your plan is a short conversation rather than an investigation.

Screening: What We Can Document

Here is ours, stated plainly rather than dressed up.

  • Identity verification before placement
  • A police certificate from the assistant’s country of residence, available on request
  • An NDA on your own form, signed whenever one is requested
  • Insurance coverage carried by VRA, which we will confirm in writing
  • A named individual assigned to your office, rather than a pool or a rotating team — one person your file can identify
  • Four weeks of instructor-led training completed before the assistant touches client work

If your program sets a particular screening standard, tell us at the start and we will tell you what we can document against it. The next section is the part that usually matters more anyway: not how thoroughly a stranger was checked, but what the access you granted would actually allow.

How this works: VRA virtual assistants provide administrative and operational support under the direction of the client. They do not provide legal, financial, or other advice requiring professional licensure. Access and permissions are determined by the client and are subject to the client’s own security, confidentiality, and compliance procedures.

Scope the Access Rather Than Hope About It

The question a reviewer is really asking is not how thoroughly a stranger was screened. It is how much damage the access you granted would allow. Those are different questions, and the second one you control.

An assistant working the stages where the time actually goes — curative follow-up, status updates to agents and lenders, closing package assembly, recording and post-closing — needs the production system, the file, the document repository and a shared inbox. They do not need escrow accounting. They do not need disbursement. They do not need the wire system, and they should not have it.

Provisioned that way, a remote assistant is a narrower NPI exposure than a local hire given full system rights on their first morning, because the permissions were deliberate instead of inherited. Set alongside that: permissions you set and revoke, one identifiable person rather than a team, and a scope of work in writing. That is a vendor file entry a reviewer can read in two minutes.

Pillar 2: The Line That Does Not Move

Pillar 2 asks that escrow trust account transactions be conducted by authorized employees, and that reconciliation be performed by someone who holds no signing or disbursing authority. A remote assistant sits outside both roles, and that is deliberate rather than a limitation we are conceding.

So: no signing authority, no disbursing authority, no wire release. The settlement statement figures and the approval of the statement belong to your escrow officer. Where your own procedures allow it, the clerical work around the edges — reporting, filing, assembling what a reconciliation needs — can be arranged, and the person performing the reconciliation remains someone of yours with no authority to move money.

Questions Worth Asking Any Remote Provider

If you are comparing options, these five surface the difference quickly, and they are worth asking us too.

  • Is it one named person, or a team? A vendor file wants a name. A pool is harder to scope and harder to screen
  • What exactly is screened, and can you see the document? “Fully vetted” is not an answer a reviewer can file
  • What does the provider say its assistants decide? Any provider claiming its assistants confirm a clear title, issue a policy, or negotiate terms is describing licensed work, and that is a signal about how carefully the rest was written
  • Who controls permissions? If the provider provisions access rather than you, your plan is no longer the standard
  • What happens to access when someone leaves? Revocation should be a switch you hold

Where Should a Title Team Start?

Most title offices do not start with a compliance question. They start with a stage that is hurting, usually curative follow-up or post-closing, and the compliance question arrives second. Both are easier answered together: the stage-by-stage walk from order opening to recording shows what the work is, and the title and escrow service page covers the production work, the systems and the access model in one place. The account of one compliance leader going from one assistant to three is the version with a real office in it.

Questions Title Teams Ask About Compliance

Can a title agency use a virtual assistant under ALTA Best Practices?

Nothing in the framework prohibits it. What the framework does is put the decision on the agency: Pillar 3 asks you to maintain a written information security program, and the 2023 revisions ask that the policies of service providers with access to your NPI or your information systems be consistent with that program. So the question is not whether outsourcing is allowed, it is whether the access you grant, and what you can document about the person you grant it to, fits the plan you already wrote. That is your assessment and your underwriter’s to make, not ours.

Which ALTA pillar covers third-party service providers?

Pillar 3, Protecting NPI. Vendor management is often described as a pillar of its own, and it is not — the seven pillars are Licensing, Escrow Trust Accounting, Protecting NPI, Settlement Processes, Title Opinions, Insurance Coverage and Consumer Complaints. The 2023 version 4.0 revisions put service providers inside Pillar 3, alongside third-party software, automated processes, APIs and add-ins, all of which it asks to be consistent with your written information security program.

Are VRA assistants background checked?

Assistants are identity verified before placement, and a police certificate from the assistant’s country of residence can be provided on request. If your written information security program sets a particular screening standard, tell us at the start: we will tell you what we can document against it, and scope the access to match.

What access does a remote assistant need to NPI?

Whatever you provision, and the useful move is to provision narrowly. An assistant working curative follow-up, status updates and post-closing needs the production system, the file, the document repository and the shared inbox. They do not need escrow accounting, disbursement, or the wire system. Permissions are set by you, are subject to your own security, confidentiality, and compliance procedures, and are revocable at any time, which means the exposure is a decision you make rather than a condition you inherit.

Can a remote assistant work in our escrow accounting system?

Not in any way that touches funds. VRA assistants have no signing authority and no disbursing authority, and they do not release wires. Pillar 2 asks that escrow trust account transactions be conducted by authorized employees, and that reconciliation be performed by someone without signing or disbursing authority — a remote assistant sits outside both of those roles by design. Reporting, filing and reconciling-adjacent clerical work can be arranged where your own procedures allow it.

What documentation can you give us for our vendor file?

Identity verification, a signed NDA on your form whenever one is requested, confirmation of VRA’s insurance coverage, a police certificate on request, the name of the individual assigned to your office, and a description of the training the assistant completed before touching client work. If your assessment needs something beyond that list, ask and we will tell you whether we have it.
