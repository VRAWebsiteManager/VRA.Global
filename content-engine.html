A Video and Podcast Producer for Your Whole Market Center

The short answer: VRA places a video and podcast producer with your market center. They write the script, direct the shoot while the agent stands in your room, cut the footage and get it back to them. It is priced per engagement, out of the agent attraction and retention budget.

The agents you want are weighing you against a brokerage with no building. Most of what an office offers can be matched on paper. A room, the equipment in it and somebody whose job is making an agent’s content cannot be, because those only exist where there is an office.

And the help agents actually want is with the part they cannot do alone. They know they should be on camera. Almost none of them are, and it is not for want of trying — it is the script nobody writes, the shoot nobody schedules and the edit that eats a Saturday.

The Problem

Why Agents Are Not on Camera

Making one video is three separate jobs. Someone has to decide what it is about and write it. Someone has to run the shoot so the agent is lit, framed and not reading from their phone. Someone has to cut it, caption it and get it out while it is still relevant.

An agent who is good at one of those is rarely good at all three, and the one they are worst at is the one that stops them. A week spent making one mediocre video is a week not spent on listings, which is why the habit dies in month two even when the intention was real.

Scope

What the Producer Does

One person carrying all three jobs, on a schedule.

Writes the script

  • Built from the agent’s own market knowledge, not generic content
  • Listing tours, neighborhood guides, market updates
  • The questions buyers and sellers actually keep asking

Directs the shoot remotely

  • The producer runs the session while the agent is on camera at your office
  • Framing, lighting and delivery handled live, not fixed afterwards
  • No one at the office has to learn to film

Edits, delivers and schedules

  • Finished pieces plus short cuts for social
  • Captions and formats for each platform
  • Turnaround the agent does not have to chase

Podcasts and interviews run on the same room and the same producer, recorded at the office and delivered ready to use. What a podcast is actually for goes through why agents do it.

More on what keeps agents filming is in turning office events into content. The second video is the one that decides whether this works. Agents rarely stop because the first one was bad; they stop because making the next one went back to being their job. Some offices book a series rather than one session at a time. The producer arrives with a bank of topics, so what gets made next is settled before anyone walks into the room.

What It Takes

What Your Office Actually Needs

Less than most leaders assume. A plain white wall is enough to begin with. An office that wants a dedicated set with backdrops and lighting can build one, and some do, but that is a choice about how the room looks rather than a condition of getting started.

The production capability travels with the producer. The room is yours, built to whatever standard you want it, and it stays yours.

The Case

The Two Questions a Team Leader Is Answering

How do I make the agents I already have more productive? Buyers and sellers look an agent up before they call, and an agent who shows up consistently is already familiar by the time the phone rings. Almost none of them manage it alone, because it is the third video that gets skipped rather than the first. Removing the writing, the filming and the editing turns a project back into a fifteen-minute ask, and that is the difference between agents who intend to be on camera and agents who are.

How do I attract agents who will be productive? This is the part that compounds. It attracts the agents who recognize what it is for. An agent who lights up at a studio and a producer is telling you something about how they intend to work, and that is a better filter than any recruiting pitch. An agent at most offices leaves a builder presentation with a page of notes; an agent with a room and a producer leaves with a finished video. An office can go further and attach a series to the moment a recruited agent arrives, so the offer is visible before they have moved a single file.

The same producer also makes the office’s own content — recruiting pieces, leadership messages, agent testimonials, podcast episodes, and the promotion that fills a room for an event or a class. A paid class like BOLD does not fill because somebody announced it once; it takes weeks of steady promotion, and that is video somebody has to make. It can run the other way as well: an office that brings a builder or a lender in to present can put those agents straight in front of the camera afterwards, while they still have something specific to say.

And it is a reason to stay. A room, the equipment in it and somebody whose job is making an agent’s content are things an agent cannot assemble for themselves, and they exist only where there is an office. For an agent who wants to be in front of their market consistently, that is a tangible benefit rather than a number, and it is one a remote brokerage has no way to deliver.

All three are the team leader’s job, and none of them is a back office problem. That is why this comes out of the agent attraction, productivity and retention budget.

FAQ

Questions Market Center Leaders Ask

What the producer covers, what your office needs, and how it is priced.

What does the producer actually do?

Three jobs that are usually three different people. The producer writes the script from the agent's own market knowledge, directs the shoot remotely while the agent is on camera at your office, then edits the footage and delivers the finished piece along with short cuts for social. What they do not need from the agent is preparation, equipment or editing time, which is what decides whether that agent is still filming in month four.

Does this cover podcasts, or only video?

Both. The same room and the same producer handle podcast and interview recording, and the finished episode comes back ready to use.

Do we have to build a studio first?

No. A plain white wall is enough to start. An office that wants to invest in a dedicated set can, and some do, but it is a choice rather than a precondition. The production capability travels with the producer, not with the room.

Who comes up with what the videos are about?

The agent brings the market knowledge and the producer turns it into a script. Listing tours, neighborhood guides, market updates, answers to the questions buyers and sellers keep asking. The agent sounds like themselves because the substance is theirs.

How is this different from a regular VRA assistant?

Every other assistant VRA places arrives ready to learn your job description. This one arrives with the job description already written. VRA defines the role, recruits against it and trains to it.

What does it cost?

Pricing is set per engagement, because VRA writes and staffs the role. Book a call and we will walk through what your office wants to produce and what it takes to run it.

How often do videos go out?

As often as the office puts agents in front of the camera. Agents can book a session, and the office can tie sessions to things already happening — a builder presentation, a market shift, a milestone. An office can also tie a series to a moment that matters, such as a recruited agent arriving, which turns one booking into a run of them.

We are competing with a cloud brokerage. Does this help?

Yes. It is one of the few things that does, because it cannot be delivered remotely. A room, the equipment and a producer who makes an agent’s content are not things an agent assembles for themselves, and they exist to put that agent in front of their market more consistently. It gives a retention conversation something tangible to point at rather than a list of intentions.

Is this only for the agents, or for the office too?

Both. The same producer makes the office’s own content alongside the agents’ — recruiting pieces, leadership messages, agent testimonials, podcast episodes, and the promotion that fills a room for an event or a class. It runs the other way too: an office that brings builders or lenders in to present can walk those agents straight into the room afterwards, so the event becomes content the same afternoon.
